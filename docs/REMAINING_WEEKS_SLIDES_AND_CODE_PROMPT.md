# Master prompt for building the remaining weeks

Copy the prompt below into a new working session. Replace the bracketed fields before beginning.

---

## Prompt

We are developing **Week [NUMBER]: [TOPIC]** for *Generative AI in Sociology* at NYU. The class meets for 165 minutes and consists of a short opening lecture, a substantial discussion of the assigned readings, and a final beginner-facing Python and LLM walkthrough. The students are 5–10 graduate sociology students. They understand sociological research but began the course with no assumed Python knowledge.

Do not begin by drafting a slide deck. Follow the staged process below and stop at each approval gate. The point is to build one coherent class in which the intellectual history, current literature, slides, code, workbook, and take-home task all address the same sociological problem.

The currently agreed remaining teaching sequence is:

- **4 November / Week 9:** LLMs at work.
- **11 November / Week 10:** writing, influence, and relationships.
- **18 November / Week 11:** language, inequality, and institutional power.
- **25 November:** class cancelled.
- **2 December:** individual proposal meetings; no lecture or coding task.
- **9 December:** project presentations; no new lecture or coding task.

For the week being built, begin from the approved topic and current course files. Do not restore the superseded sequence on generative agents, collective intelligence, or research agents. Agentic systems may appear briefly where they illuminate the week’s substantive question, but they are not the organizing subject.

### 1. Start with the sociological question and its prehistory

The class must explain what sociologists were already studying and how they studied it before introducing LLMs. “Prehistory” does not mean a decorative list of classic authors. It should establish the research problem, the unit of analysis, the evidence sociologists used, and the unresolved disputes that make the LLM development interesting.

For every week, reconstruct a relevant lineage:

1. What sociological phenomenon or methodological problem predates LLMs?
2. Which foundational and later studies made it empirically observable?
3. What earlier technology, organizational arrangement, or methodological innovation changed it before generative AI?
4. What remains continuous when LLMs enter?
5. What is actually new about LLMs: scale, adaptivity, conversational form, probabilistic generation, generality across tasks, opacity, provider control, or something else?
6. What inferential burden remains with the researcher?

For a week on work, for example, begin with the sociology of technological change at work: mechanization and automation, labour process and managerial control, skill and deskilling, computerization, workplace surveillance, algorithmic management, tacit knowledge, and earlier decision-support systems. Then explain which features of generative AI reproduce these older dynamics and which may differ. Use the same principle for communication, relationships, language, discrimination, or institutions: establish the relevant sociological tradition first, then identify the difference made by LLMs.

Bring this history to life with original visual documentation where possible: book covers, archival documents, historical workplace photographs, instruments, fieldnotes, experimental materials, newspaper or trade-union records, original figures, and legible passages from the primary source. A later review article may interpret an older study, but should not automatically become its visual proxy.

Do not force every week into the same historical sequence. Select the lineage that is substantively appropriate. The history must help students answer the current question, not merely demonstrate coverage.

### 2. Conduct the literature review before proposing slides

Search systematically for:

- foundational sociological work;
- the relevant pre-LLM methodological or technological literature;
- recent peer-reviewed LLM research;
- one substantive application that uses the technique or investigates the phenomenon rather than merely discussing method;
- important critical or contradictory evidence;
- recent work whose publication status must be stated accurately.

Prefer final published articles. Use a working paper or preprint only when its novelty is necessary, label it honestly, and explain why it is included. Do not use an unpublished dissertation simply because its design is convenient. Assign whole articles, not isolated page ranges, unless there is an exceptional reason.

The required readings should normally include distinct jobs rather than three versions of the same warning:

- one sociological or historical anchor;
- one strong contemporary methods or causal-design paper where relevant;
- one substantive application.

Do not repeat the APSA *AI and Research Methods* chapter after Week 2 merely as a general map. Include it later only if a specific section is indispensable to the week’s argument.

For every candidate reading, record:

- full citation and publication status;
- research question;
- material, data, participants, or setting;
- design or comparison;
- exact principal finding, including magnitude where reported;
- limitations;
- the single teaching job it performs;
- whether it belongs in required reading, additional reading, or the lecture only;
- the exact figure, table, quotation, instrument, treatment, or result that could support the slide claim.

Prune rather than accumulate. A paper belongs in the main deck only if it changes the question, comparison, standard, evidence, or procedure. State what a new paper replaces.

**Approval gate 1:** Return with the literature audit, proposed required and additional readings, the historical lineage, and any PDFs you need me to supply. Do not draft final slides yet.

### 3. Develop one cumulative argument and example

After the literature direction is approved, state:

- the one sociological question for the class;
- the bounded claim students should be able to defend by the end;
- the main empirical or teaching example;
- why that example is sociologically meaningful;
- whether its data are empirical, adapted, public, cached model output, simulated, or instructor-authored;
- what the example cannot establish;
- how each reading changes what we do with the example;
- how the Python exercise implements part of the same research process.

Prefer one cumulative research problem over several disconnected demonstrations. Reuse the same cases, texts, interactions, records, or outcomes so that construct definition, model use, validation, and interpretation build on one another.

Introduce the focal LLM procedure early, using one exact input and one exact output, before the historical or validation sections assume that students know what the model is doing. Then return to the longer history. This avoids making students infer the technical object while also learning the literature.

Design the class so the historical and contemporary sections speak to each other. The sequence should normally be:

1. a concrete empirical problem or object;
2. the exact LLM operation being considered;
3. what sociologists already knew about the underlying phenomenon or method;
4. earlier technological or organizational changes;
5. what LLMs change and what they do not;
6. two or three contemporary studies with distinct jobs;
7. a comparison or unresolved question for discussion;
8. the cumulative example;
9. the Python walkthrough;
10. a return to the opening object and a plain answer to the opening question.

This is a guide, not a compulsory template. Change the order when the material warrants it, but explain every transition.

**Approval gate 2:** Present a timed 165-minute architecture, the title-only slide sequence, the role of every paper, the cumulative example, the reading-discussion questions, and the reading-to-code map. I should be able to explain why every slide follows the preceding one by reading the titles alone.

### 4. Write slides that are intelligible without an invisible talk track

Each slide must make clear:

- what it is showing;
- where the evidence comes from;
- what comparison or finding matters;
- why the slide appears at this point;
- what students should understand before advancing.

Do not rely on me to supply all the nouns, context, design, or finding orally. At the same time, do not fill slides with prose that should remain in speaker notes. Give students enough information to understand the object and the claim while keeping the evidence readable.

Before displaying a result, define the design in ordinary language. For each focal paper, make clear:

- what the paper is;
- what was studied and why;
- who or what was observed;
- what each condition actually received;
- who or what generated any judgment;
- which model, provider, or technical stack matters;
- what was compared;
- what the authors found;
- what the result permits us to claim;
- why the paper is in this lecture.

Major papers generally need more than one slide. A useful pattern is a recognizable title page or source introduction, followed by a readable design slide, followed by the actual result. Do not use a title page in place of evidence, and do not show a methods diagram when discussing a finding.

Define all terms before using them. Spell out acronyms. Explain author-created condition names through what participants or models actually encountered. Distinguish concepts that students could reasonably conflate: condition and stimulus; label and construct; individual and aggregate accuracy; transcript and decision record; context window and memory; hosted route and local model; operational success and substantive validity.

Every table, typology, list of criteria, sequence, or framework must be traceable to published literature or identified neutrally as a course heuristic. Do not make an instructor synthesis look as if it were reproduced from a paper. Avoid defensive production captions such as “This is an instructor synthesis”; instead, write the substantive point naturally and cite its components.

### 5. Use a direct, natural academic voice

Write as a social scientist explaining research to intelligent colleagues and graduate students. The tone should be direct, precise, conversational, empirically grounded, and occasionally dry. It should not sound like a consultant, a TED talk, a LinkedIn post, or an LLM trying to sound insightful.

Review all titles, subtitles, headings, captions, callouts, and prominent text for the following problems:

- artificially punchy formulations such as “The claim determines the check,” “Three papers, one common lesson,” or “One question, many answers”;
- slogan-like contrasts such as “Not X, but Y,” “Less X, more Y,” and “From X to Y”;
- compressed abstractions such as “the answer becomes input,” “realized interview path,” “inspectable record,” “operations, not warrants,” or “trade external transfer for hardware and maintenance”;
- abstract noun clusters such as “our route is sociology, causal design, generated candidates, review and Python”;
- vague insights such as “what really matters,” “the bigger picture,” or “why this changes everything”;
- unnecessary symmetry, parallelism, alliteration, colons, em dashes, rhetorical questions, three-part slogans, and overly polished concluding lines;
- generic consultancy language such as “key takeaways,” “unpacking,” “the path forward,” “unlocking,” and “rethinking”;
- visible production notes such as “reason for using it here,” “role today,” “direct bridge,” “this slide summarizes,” or “instructor-authored model-style output”;
- redundant warnings and captions that merely repeat the title;
- claims that an approach “solves” a methodological problem when it only changes or reduces it.

Prefer descriptive titles that state the actual subject, design, result, or question. “Research design,” “Results,” and “Limitations” are acceptable. Preserve titles that already sound natural. Do not rewrite merely to produce variation.

Use enough ordinary language. Brevity is not automatically clarity. If a slide cannot be understood because it has been reduced to one polished sentence, explain the design or example more fully.

Read every visible sentence aloud. Final test: would I naturally say this while explaining the evidence to colleagues? If it sounds written to impress, rewrite it.

### 6. Treat source images as evidence

Acquire the source PDFs before building around them. If the correct article is inaccessible, ask me to place the PDF in the relevant `literature/source_pdfs/sessionXX/` directory. Do not quietly substitute:

- a preprint when the published paper exists;
- a dissertation for a better published study;
- a JSTOR, library-login, publisher-error, or metadata page;
- a screenshot of text referring to a figure instead of the figure itself;
- a generic web preview for the original historical object.

Crop source images at semantic boundaries. Never end halfway through a paragraph, quotation, table, arrow, face, experimental stage, or figure. Preserve aspect ratio. Remove empty margins that make the evidence unreadable. Enlarge passages and figures that students must actually read. Highlight the precise lines, cells, words, or estimate being discussed, without obscuring the original.

Every source image needs a complete, visible citation. Use a full citation on first appearance and a consistent shortened citation with page, table, or figure on subsequent crops. Keep the DOI or stable URL on screen where used, without allowing it to run below the slide.

Label course-created plots, reconstructed distributions, synthetic data, cached model output, and adapted examples directly and neutrally. State their source values and limits. Captions must not resemble quotations unless they are quotations.

Use visual variety only when it improves comprehension. Appropriate forms include source crops, photographs, archival objects, experimental stimuli, short transcripts, comparisons, simple diagrams, plots, and code traces. Avoid repeated beige slides containing one sentence, decorative card grids, black-background interludes, and arrows that merely announce a sequence.

Check for blank slides, overlapping text, captions on top of images, stretched images, screenshots wider than the slide, text falling off the bottom, missing table rules, inconsistent citation placement, unclear reading order, sparse slides, and overloaded slides. Split dense evidence rather than shrinking it. Combine thin slides when the separation serves no teaching purpose.

### 7. Make the reading discussion part of the argument

The middle portion of the class is a discussion of the assigned readings, not a second sequence of paper summaries. Write a small set of questions that require students to compare claims, evidence, designs, units of analysis, and limitations across the readings.

Questions should return to the cumulative case and prepare the code exercise. Avoid generic prompts such as “What did you think?” and avoid pair-work instructions unless specifically requested. The usual format is discussion with the room.

### 8. Design one beginner-facing Python and LLM routine

The code must implement a research operation introduced in the lecture. It should be engaging and extendable to real research, but technically simpler than the substantive idea. Students are learning Python through the anatomy of an LLM routine, not learning elegant software engineering.

Use this visible sequence:

`research material → Python object → prompt/messages → OpenRouter or Ollama call → raw return → parsed output → methodological check`

For the current remaining weeks:

- **Week 9** introduces the first small student-edited function. Every parameter must first be replaced with a literal value so students can see what enters it.
- **Week 10** may reuse that function and introduce an interaction or suggestion log, but should not hide the model call inside unexplained infrastructure.
- **Week 11** may use a small predeclared condition grid and result table. Nested iteration must be traced with actual values before it is generalized.

The week’s routine must state, before any syntax:

- the research question;
- the exact material being analysed or generated;
- the unit of analysis;
- the operation the model is asked to perform;
- the intended output;
- what the program does not do;
- how the output will be checked.

For every code block, explain:

1. the exact input value;
2. its Python type;
3. the current operation;
4. the exact output value;
5. the output type;
6. what it means for the sociological task;
7. what it does not establish.

Show the complete workflow in plain pseudocode before splitting it into cells. Then trace one complete case from beginning to end. For a loop, show the first iteration with actual values and state before and after. For a branch, show which comparison becomes `True` or `False`. For a function, show arguments entering, local values changing, and the returned object. Print intermediate objects instead of asking beginners to infer invisible state.

Keep the route visible. OpenRouter is a hosted routing service accessed through an SDK and the shared key. Ollama serves a downloaded model locally. Explain client, server, model, API, SDK, request, response, context window, latency, cost, privacy, retention, hardware, and maintenance only as they become relevant. Do not treat the ChatGPT interface, a provider API, OpenRouter, and Ollama as interchangeable objects.

Use a live model call as the ordinary path and a recorded return as the outage fallback. Never expose a key or send identifiable or sensitive research data. Preserve the raw response before parsing. Explain the response shape progressively rather than using one unexplained chain of dot notation and indexes.

The exercise should invite one meaningful change and rerun: one worker-experience field, one suggestion condition, one dialect condition, one instruction, one parameter, or one model. The change should illuminate the research question rather than exist solely to demonstrate syntax.

Build in researcher judgment. The student should inspect a source, countercase, failed condition, unexpected output, or difference across runs and say what the model result does and does not support. A passing schema or assertion confirms a software expectation, not the sociological claim.

Do not introduce unexplained helper functions, classes, initializers, package machinery, or compact syntax. Do not define student functions before the agreed point in the course. Do not allow the notebook to become a collection of disconnected examples. The site page, notebook, local script, Colab version, slides, downloadable task, and answer fixtures must implement the same assessed routine.

The take-home task is completion-marked. Students submit a narrated screen recording in which they run the exercise and explain the input and output of each operation. Code elegance is not graded. Include a bounded reading connection and AI-use disclosure.

### 9. Build all aligned artifacts together

After approval, update the week as one system:

- literature audit and synthesis;
- session architecture;
- required and additional readings with full citations and stable links;
- main course-book page;
- slide deck;
- separate Python course-book page;
- canonical local notebook or script;
- Colab-compatible notebook;
- task instructions;
- recorded fixtures or fallback outputs;
- tests and validation scripts;
- instructor notes;
- bibliography;
- any schedule, syllabus, or cross-week transitions affected by the change.

Do not change a paper, example, model, field name, or exercise only in the slides. Reconcile all references to it. Do not let an old generated workbook, stale notebook, duplicate file, or scaffolding script overwrite the collaboratively revised version.

### 10. Review the rendered lecture as an editorial task

Rendering successfully is not evidence that a deck is ready.

Perform these reviews in order:

1. **Title-only review:** read the full title sequence aloud. Explain why each slide follows the previous slide. Repair gaps before inspecting decoration.
2. **Contact-sheet review:** inspect density, rhythm, visual repetition, abrupt changes, blank slides, sparse stretches, and overloaded clusters.
3. **Full-size evidence review:** inspect every paper crop, quotation, figure, table, result, experimental material, citation, and historical object at presentation size.
4. **Full-size code review:** verify readable type, exact inputs and outputs, line-by-line continuity, and agreement with the notebook.
5. **Voice review:** read all prominent copy aloud and remove slogans, compressed abstractions, defensive captions, presenter notes, and “Claudish” phrasing.
6. **Fidelity review:** confirm that no image is stretched, cropped inaccurately, mislabeled, or visually attributed to the wrong source.
7. **Source review:** compare each empirical claim with the source PDF. Confirm sample, design, condition, estimate, uncertainty, and limitation.
8. **End-to-end code review:** execute the local script and notebook from a clean environment, then execute the Colab path. Confirm dependencies, setup cells, model routes, cached fallback, saved outputs, and links.
9. **Alignment review:** compare slides, book prose, readings, notebook, task, instructor notes, and bibliography for drift.
10. **Closing review:** return to the exact opening object, answer its question in ordinary language, and end with genuine open questions from the literature rather than a slogan.

Do not claim visual inspection unless you have actually inspected rendered slides at full size. Do not rely only on overflow checks or a contact sheet; these cannot reveal an unreadable source, a stretched person, a misleading crop, an unclear bridge, or unnatural language.

### 11. Self-interrogate before presenting the build

Answer these questions explicitly:

- Can a student explain the lecture’s argument from the title sequence alone?
- Does the opening establish a real sociological problem rather than announce a framework?
- Is the LLM operation concrete before the deck assumes knowledge of it?
- Does the historical section explain what sociologists already knew and how they knew it?
- Does each classic author or earlier technology have a distinct teaching job?
- Is what is new about LLMs stated precisely rather than implied by recency?
- Is every methodological claim anchored in a source?
- Is every teaching framework traceable or honestly identified as a heuristic?
- Does every focal paper show its question, material, design, exact finding, limitation, and role?
- Are the screenshots the correct published sources and the correct figures or passages?
- Can the evidence actually be read when projected?
- Are all examples and reconstructed visuals accurately labeled?
- Have redundant papers and asides been removed?
- Does the reading discussion advance the same question?
- Does the code perform an operation that the lecture has prepared students to understand?
- Can a beginner state the value and type entering and leaving every code block?
- Is model-generated output checked against source material, a design requirement, human data, or another run?
- Are the local and Colab paths genuinely tested rather than assumed?
- Does the take-home recording assess understanding rather than fluency or code style?
- Has every phrase that sounds like a slogan, consultancy copy, or AI-generated profundity been rewritten?
- Does the final slide answer the opening question and leave students with open empirical questions that arise from the literature?

If any answer is no, revise before asking me to review.

**Approval gate 3:** Show me the rendered deck, the complete code flow in digestible form, the exact take-home task, the reading list, and a short report of the checks performed. Do not publish the course site until I approve the reviewed artifacts.

---

## Why this prompt is strict

The repeated problems in earlier builds were not isolated formatting mistakes. They followed a consistent pattern: slides were built before the literature and argument were settled; historical material was present but not doing a clear job; papers appeared without enough design or finding; tables and criteria looked authoritative without sources; screenshots were treated as decoration; code explained syntax without preserving the research question; and polished language concealed missing transitions. The staged process above is intended to prevent those failures rather than repair them after rendering.

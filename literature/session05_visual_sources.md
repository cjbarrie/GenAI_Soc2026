# Session 5 visual sources — narrating the craft of sociological experiments

**Status:** Source shortlist completed on 2026-08-21 and publisher-source corrections completed on 2026-08-24. Every crop must be checked against the original and cited in the slide notes.

## Visual argument

The images should show that an experiment is made out of practical objects and decisions:

> artificial social setting → performed encounter → written or recorded vignette → matched application → generated candidates → human validation → retained treatment set

This is more useful than a procession of researcher portraits. A portrait can identify an author, but it rarely shows students how a social-scientific comparison was constructed.

## Core visual sequence

### Opening map: what carries the treatment?

The first orientation slide should distinguish five objects without becoming a dense typology:

- **standardized partner** — Berger, Cohen, and Zelditch's expectation-states tradition;
- **human confederate in a status experiment** — Ridgeway and Erickson's deferential and nondeferential partners;
- **human confederate** — Piliavin, Rodin, and Piliavin's subway experiment;
- **written or recorded vignette** — Emerson et al. and Krysan et al. on neighborhood preferences;
- **trained audit tester** — Pager's employment study;
- **artificial social system** — Music Lab.

Use one documentary fragment for each: an actual task sequence, the subway paper, the actual residential vignette, the Pager paper or field protocol, and the Music Lab interface. This is an orientation image, not five miniature paper summaries. Its single question is: **what did participants actually encounter?**

### 1. Salganik, Dodds, and Watts's Music Lab

**Sources:** [Princeton Music Lab project page](https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/musiclab.shtml), the [author-hosted 2006 paper](https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/salganik_dodds_watts06_full.pdf), and the [author-hosted follow-up with interface screenshots](https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/salganik_watts09.pdf).

**Candidate images:**

- the original Music Lab interface showing forty-eight unfamiliar songs;
- the ranked social-influence interface showing download counts;
- the design diagram separating an independent condition from eight parallel social-influence worlds;
- the outcome plot showing that the song rankings diverged across worlds.

**Why it is useful:** This is a genuinely sociological experiment with a visible experimental setting. Salganik, Dodds, and Watts recruited 14,341 participants into an artificial cultural market. Participants could listen to, rate, and download the same forty-eight unfamiliar songs. In the independent condition they did not see other participants' choices. In the social-influence condition they saw download counts from participants in their own randomly assigned world. The researchers could therefore examine how social information affected inequality and unpredictability in cultural success.

**Narrative line:**

> The website was not just where the experiment happened. What it displayed about other people's choices was the treatment.

**Recommended use:** Use two adjacent slides. The first shows the design and establishes that the songs and starting conditions were shared across worlds. The second shows the actual interfaces and asks students to identify the intended difference. Include the divergent rankings as the substantive payoff: the same starting set did not produce the same hits.

**Teaching connection:** Music Lab makes the construction problem easy to see. The researchers had to choose the songs, build the interfaces, decide which social information appeared, create separate worlds, and record listening and downloading. That prepares the move to factorial surveys: a written vignette is another deliberately constructed representation of a social situation.

**Reuse note:** Prefer screenshots and diagrams from the author-hosted papers or project page, keep their original proportions, and cite the exact paper and figure. The [Bit by Bit companion page](https://www.bitbybitbook.com/en/1st-ed/running-experiments/exp-advice/zero-variable-cost/) is useful for locating the design and interface figures, but the slide crop should come from the original source where possible.

### 2. Piliavin, Rodin, and Piliavin's subway field experiment

**Sources:** [Original article record and DOI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5359227/) and the article, “Good Samaritanism: An Underground Phenomenon?” (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1969).

**Experimental objects:**

- an express subway car used as a recurring field setting;
- a four-person team comprising a victim, a helping model, and two observers;
- a cane or alcohol bottle used to convey the victim's apparent condition;
- a timed collapse and, on selected trials, a timed intervention by the model;
- observer records of help, latency, movement, and comments.

**Why it is useful:** The study shows what a confederate design requires beyond the abstract condition labels. A person has to enact the treatment repeatedly, other team members must occupy credible positions, timing must be coordinated, and observations must be made without reorganizing the scene.

**Narrative line:**

> Here the treatment was not a paragraph on a screen. A research team had to perform it on a moving subway car.

**Recommended visual treatment:** Show the actual title and methods page beside a simple, explicitly labelled course reconstruction of the car: victim, model, two observers, critical area, and approximate event sequence. Do not use a generic photograph as if it documents a particular trial.

**Ethical and inferential caution:** Passengers did not provide advance consent, the staged emergency could create distress, and performances could vary across teams and trials. Use these issues to show why realism, standardization, and ethics can pull in different directions.

### 3. Ridgeway and Erickson's confederate status experiment

**Sources:** [Official AJS article page](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/318966) and an [accessible copy of the article](https://ndg.asc.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/318966.pdf).

**Why it is useful:** This is a direct sociology example of a treatment carried through interaction. Confederates used constant choices and arguments but presented them in either a confident, nondeferential manner or an uncertain, deferential manner. Multiple confederates were assigned across conditions. Coders who did not know the conditions reviewed recordings to check whether the enacted behavior matched the intended manipulation.

**Recommended visual treatment:** Show the AJS title page so the disciplinary location is unmistakable. Beside it, show a tightly cropped methods passage defining the two interaction styles and the footnote describing the coded recording check. A simple two-column course annotation can mark **held constant** (choices and arguments) and **varied** (deferential or nondeferential delivery).

**Narrative line:**

> The confederates said the same things. The experiment varied how confidently and deferentially they interacted.

**Connection to generated agents:** A prompt can ask a model to adopt an interaction style, but that does not establish that the style remained comparable across conversations. The confederate-performance check supplies the historical analogue for auditing agent behavior.

### 4. Berger, Cohen, and Zelditch's standardized task situation — brief inset

**Sources:** [Open Stanford Sociology technical report](https://oaktrust.library.tamu.edu/bitstreams/2b787424-7e68-4b35-8c00-2d3ef08847dc/download) and the [Social Psychology Quarterly example of a later standardized situation](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0190272519868988).

**Why it is useful:** Expectation-states experiments manipulated what participants knew about an ostensible partner and controlled how that partner appeared to respond during a joint task. The setting was designed to measure whether participants stayed with their own answer or deferred after a disagreement. This is a central sociological laboratory tradition, not a borrowed psychology illustration.

**Recommended visual treatment:** Use the later paper's actual disagreement-trial figure because it is more readable than the early technical-report prose. Pair it with one sentence locating the design in Berger, Cohen, and Zelditch's status-characteristics research program.

**Narrative line:**

> The participant believed another person was responding. The research design controlled the information and the replies attributed to that partner.

**Connection to Session 6:** A standardized partner follows a fixed experimental sequence. An LLM agent can respond to what a participant says, so the resulting treatment may diverge across conversations.

### 5. An actual factorial-survey vignette

**Source:** [Wallander, “25 Years of Factorial Surveys in Sociology”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0049089X09000192), Figure 1.

**Image:** The paper reproduces the introductory statement, six experimentally varied dimensions, their possible levels, and the respondent's rating task from a study of racial residential segregation.

**Why it is useful:** Students can see a treatment as a piece of text participants actually encountered. The figure makes it possible to point to:

- the fixed instructions;
- the values inserted into a particular vignette;
- the wider set of possible levels;
- the outcome question.

**Narrative line:**

> The vignette reads like a short social description, but the researcher sees it as one combination drawn from a much larger design.

**Recommended use:** Show the actual figure at readable size, then reveal a course overlay or adjacent list that names each dimension. Do not crop away the rating task; without it, students cannot see what the vignette was built to elicit.

**Reuse note:** Publisher figure; use a limited paper screenshot for classroom criticism and teaching with full citation.

**Substantive source behind the displayed vignette:** [Emerson, Yancey, and Chai, “Does Race Matter in Residential Segregation?”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/000312240106600607). The over-the-telephone factorial experiment asked 1,663 white respondents about purchasing a home while varying neighborhood racial composition and other features for which race might serve as a proxy.

**Why naming this matters:** The figure should never be presented as an anonymous example of vignette syntax. It was constructed to answer a sociological question about segregation, neighborhood choice, race, crime, and housing value.

### 6. Krysan and colleagues' neighborhood video experiment — optional medium bridge

**Source:** [Krysan et al., “Does Race Matter in Neighborhood Preferences? Results from a Video Experiment”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/599248).

**Why it is useful:** The study moves from a spoken or written neighborhood description to a visual representation. It therefore gives the session a pre-generative example of why researchers change media and what that costs methodologically. The video can make the scenario more concrete, but the whole recorded scene becomes evidence: buildings, upkeep, people, traffic, camera position, editing, and sound.

**Recommended use:** A small aside after the actual Wallander/Emerson vignette, ideally using an author-provided treatment still or paper figure. If the original treatment imagery cannot be sourced cleanly, show only the actual paper page and describe the move to video; do not substitute stock neighborhood footage.

### 7. Correll, Benard, and Paik's matched application design

**Source:** [Stanford Sociology publication page](https://sociology.stanford.edu/publications/getting-job-there-motherhood-penalty) and the original AJS article linked from that page.

**Available visual evidence:** The paper/title page clearly identifies the source. The public page explains that participants evaluated application materials for equally qualified, same-gender applicants who differed in parental status.

**Recommended visual treatment:**

- show the actual paper title/author page;
- beside it, use a clearly labelled **course schematic based on the study design**, not a fake screenshot of the original résumés;
- show two application records with common qualifications and one visible parenthood cue;
- label exactly which parts are reported by the paper and which are simplified for teaching.

**Why not use generic résumé stock imagery:** It would make the experiment look illustrative rather than constructed and could falsely imply that the slide reproduces the actual materials.

**Narrative line:**

> “Equally qualified” is something the researchers had to produce on paper. The design depends on which details they matched and how parenthood became visible.

**Reuse note:** Use the actual article page under classroom fair use. The course schematic is original but must cite the study and state that it is not the original application.

### 8. Pager's audit-study field procedure — optional supporting image

**Sources:** [Pager, “The Mark of a Criminal Record”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1086/374403), the [Harvard memorial account of the fieldwork](https://officeofthesecretary.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum2291/files/2024-11/Pager%20Memorial%20Minute.pdf), and [Pager's EEOC statement](https://www.eeoc.gov/meetings/meeting-november-20-2008-employment-discrimination-faced-individuals-arrest-and-conviction/pager).

**Recommended visual treatment:** Build a restrained course schematic from documented procedural objects:

- matched résumés and work histories;
- trained tester pairs;
- rotation of the criminal-record condition within pairs;
- standardized dress and interaction;
- voicemail/callback record;
- immediate response form and narrative fieldnote.

**Why it is useful:** This shows that a field experiment requires performance, training, rotation, monitoring, and post-encounter recording. It also makes clear why uncontrolled tester differences remain a methodological concern.

**Narrative line:**

> In an audit study, the treatment is carried by both the application and a trained person moving through a real institution.

**Placement:** Optional on the audit-method overview slide. Do not give Pager a separate historical detour if Correll already makes the required point.

### 9. Schwitter's iterative visual-vignette construction

**Source:** [Schwitter, “Using Artificial Intelligence to Generate Visual Vignettes in Factorial Survey Experiments”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393251392916).

**Strongest candidate figures:**

- **Figure 1:** four images returned from one initial request;
- **Figure 3:** an initial public-speaker image and selectively edited version, including clothing and hand repairs;
- **Figure 4 or 5:** a baseline image followed by targeted variation of selected regions;
- **Table 1:** participants' reported signs that images appeared artificial or real.

**Best choice for this session:** Figure 3 followed by a small excerpt from Table 1. Figure 3 shows iterative craft without requiring a long discussion of the sensitive demographic manipulations in other figures. Table 1 shows that participants noticed faces, edges, backgrounds, poses, texture, hands, lighting, and text—not only the intended vignette dimension.

**Narrative line:**

> The model returned images, but the research material emerged through selection, regional editing, repair, and pretesting.

**Licensing:** The article states that the generated images are generally available for research under Creative Commons Attribution–NonCommercial 4.0. Confirm the figure-level credit when downloading and reproduce the full attribution in notes.

### 10. LinkedOut's picture-production and validation pipeline

**Sources used in the deck:** the typeset *Quarterly Journal of Economics* article, 140(1), 2025, DOI 10.1093/qje/qjae035, and its published online appendix. Local publisher PDFs are stored in `literature/source_pdfs/session05/`.

**Strongest candidate pages and figures:**

- **online appendix, Figure B.1:** picture creation, transformation, preselection, validation, and final pictures;
- **online appendix, Figure F.1:** CAPTCHA-style task mixing obvious fakes, real faces, and study images;
- **online appendix, Figure F.2:** individual rating task;
- **online appendix, Figure F.5:** perceived race, age, trust, intelligence, authenticity, and appearance;
- **published article, Figure II:** how twin profiles were assigned across target pools and weeks.

**Best sequence:**

1. Figure B.1 to show that 100,000 source images eventually became a much smaller validated set;
2. Figure F.1 or F.2 to show what raters actually did;
3. Figure F.5 to show the intended and unintended perceived attributes;
4. Figure 2 only if the lecture needs the later field assignment procedure.

**Narrative line:**

> The experiment did not begin with 408 ready-made profile pictures. It began with a very large image pool, a transformation procedure, automated screening, human ratings, and further selection.

**Important caution:** The paper reports that the available input pool was highly uneven across racial categories. Show this as part of the production story: the generator and its image distribution constrained which matched treatments could be created.

**Reuse note:** Use limited crops from the published article and online appendix with full attribution for classroom teaching. Preserve the faces' proportions and do not crop the images into decontextualized decorative portraits.

## Images to avoid or use only with an explicit reason

- **Hawthorne as the opening example:** historically useful, but its disciplinary location is ambiguous and its familiar methodological controversy would take the class away from the sociology of constructed treatments.
- **Milgram, Stanford Prison, and Robbers Cave:** visually familiar but primarily psychology/social psychology, ethically overdetermined, and likely to pull attention away from treatment construction in sociology.
- **Expectation-states apparatus as a full historical detour:** the tradition should appear in the opening map and a brief inset, but the later disagreement-trial figure is more legible than archival apparatus material and should be preferred.
- **Researcher portraits as the main visual:** useful for identifying an author, weak for narrating craft.
- **Generic stock photographs of surveys, résumés, or AI:** they do not document the experiment and make provenance ambiguous.
- **Recreated “original” résumés or vignettes:** a teaching schematic must be labelled as a schematic.
- **Generated period photographs of historical experiments:** these would erase the very provenance distinction the course is trying to teach.
- **Sensitive generated faces shown without their validation context:** the point is the pipeline and comparison, not the spectacle of synthetic identity.

## Build requirements

1. Capture from the authoritative archive or paper, not image-search thumbnails.
2. Save the full page/figure before creating a crop.
3. Record creator, collection/paper, date, URL, access date, license or fair-use basis, and any modification.
4. Keep the original aspect ratio.
5. State visibly whether an image is archival documentation, a paper figure, a course schematic, or a generated stimulus.
6. Never imply that a schematic reproduces original experimental materials.
7. Inspect each crop at 1280 × 720 and compare it with the source.

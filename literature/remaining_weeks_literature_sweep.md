# Literature sweep for the remaining teaching weeks

**Prepared:** 27 October 2026
**Scope:** research published or publicly available through 27 October 2026
**Purpose:** reconsider the 4, 11 and 18 November meetings; 25 November is cancelled, 2 December is reserved for individual proposal meetings, and 9 December is reserved for presentations.

## Diagnosis

The course has already covered LLMs extensively as research instruments: annotation, qualitative interpretation, multimodal evidence, treatment generation, adaptive interviewing, synthetic respondents and prediction of unobserved outcomes. The provisional Weeks 9–12 then devote three or four more meetings to interacting agents, collective intelligence, research agents and their replication.

That continuation is too narrow. It gives agentic systems more space than work, organizations, inequality, everyday communication and relationships combined. It also moves the course away from the 60:40 method/substantive balance at the point when students now have enough methodological vocabulary to study LLMs as social technologies.

## Major sociological areas not yet treated adequately

### Work, occupations and organizations

This is the largest omission. There is now evidence at several levels:

- nationally representative adoption surveys show rapid but uneven uptake and distinguish occupational exposure from actual use;
- field studies measure changes in productivity, learning, communication and after-hours work;
- organizational studies show why individual time savings need not reorganize meetings, coordination or task allocation;
- qualitative studies recover workers' experience, emotional labour and their limited power over deployment;
- studies of data work reveal the labour regimes underneath apparently autonomous systems.

Useful anchors include Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's study of 5,172 customer-support agents; Dillon et al.'s randomized field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers; Bick, Blandin and Deming's adoption surveys; Berger et al.'s qualitative study of emotional labour in an Austrian call centre; and Huang's ethnography of data-annotation work in three Chinese AI companies.

### LLM-mediated communication and influence

The course studies AI-generated messages in Week 5, but not what happens when a model is embedded inside the act of writing or conversation. Recent studies distinguish exposure to a finished AI message from accepting suggestions while composing one's own text.

Williams-Ceci et al. report two preregistered experiments with 2,582 participants in which biased autocomplete suggestions shifted reported attitudes and most users did not recognize the bias or its influence. Agarwal, Naaman and Vashistha show that suggestions from a Western-centred model changed both the efficiency and style of culturally grounded writing tasks, with Indian participants moving towards Western writing conventions. This literature makes the human–model interaction itself the treatment.

### Relationships, intimacy and companionship

The course considers AI interviewers but not ordinary users who treat conversational systems as confidants or companions. This is now an empirical literature rather than only a speculative ethics debate.

Zhang et al. analyse survey data from 1,131 Character.AI users and 4,664 chat sessions. Waldschütz et al. use randomized dyadic interactions to study closeness and disclosure. A four-week MIT/OpenAI experiment and platform-scale observational study find heterogeneous associations depending on use, attachment and duration. The evidence is not reducible to “chatbots reduce loneliness” or “companions are harmful”; selection, use pattern and platform design are central.

### Language, culture and homogenization

Models do not merely express propositions. They normalize genres, rhetorical forms and cultural styles. PNAS research comparing human and model text finds a distinctive, informationally dense style associated especially with instruction tuning. Cross-cultural writing experiments find movement towards Western styles. This connects directly to the recurring problem in the slides of recognizing formulaic “AI English,” but gives it an empirical and sociological basis.

### Stratification, dialect and institutional allocation

Bias has appeared as a validation concern in earlier weeks, but the course has not yet treated LLMs as institutions that can convert linguistic and social cues into unequal decisions.

Hofmann et al. use a matched-guise design to show that African American English can trigger negative stereotypes and consequential differences in occupational and criminal-justice recommendations even without naming race. A 361,000-resume audit finds model-specific intersectional differences in resume evaluation. Hu et al. compare social-identity biases across 77 models. These studies connect model behaviour to sociological work on discrimination, racialization and audit studies rather than treating “bias” as a generic technical defect.

### Political information, ideology and the public sphere

Week 5 already covers generated persuasive messages, but newer work examines interactive political persuasion and the political origins of model output. Human–AI dialogues have shifted candidate and ballot preferences in preregistered studies. Waight et al. combine a cross-national audit, training-data evidence, additional pretraining and commercial-model audits to show how state media control can enter model responses. This literature is valuable, but a full persuasion lecture would duplicate Week 5 unless it is framed around communication and institutional power.

### The hidden infrastructure of LLMs

Human data work, content moderation, energy, compute ownership and provider concentration are almost absent. These are central sociological questions about labour, global supply chains and organizational power. The strongest material can be integrated into the work-and-organizations meeting rather than assigned its own week.

### Knowledge production and education

The course has discussed reproducibility and qualitative researcher influence, but not yet the distribution of LLM use across academic fields, changes in scientific prose, peer review or learning. Alvero et al.'s survey of 433 recent sociology authors finds regular use concentrated more in writing than in data collection or analysis, alongside low trust and divided expectations. This is relevant, but it belongs as a short return to the students' own field rather than a full remaining week.

## Recommended three-week sequence

The three meetings should move from organizations, to interaction, to power:

### 4 November — LLMs at work

**Question:** What changes when LLM advice becomes part of a job?

**Main ideas:** adoption versus exposure; augmentation versus substitution; tacit knowledge; learning and deskilling; heterogeneous effects by experience; individual productivity versus organizational change; worker discretion; invisible data labour.

**Recommended essential readings:**

1. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025), “Generative AI at Work,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics*. A large field setting with worker-level heterogeneity, learning and customer interaction.
2. Berger et al. (2025), “Artificial Intelligence, Emotional Labor, and the Quest for Sociological and Political Imagination among Low-Skilled Workers,” *Policy and Society*. A qualitative account that keeps worker experience and power in view.

**Additional readings:** Dillon et al. (2025), Bick, Blandin and Deming (2026), Humlum and Vestergaard (2025), Huang (2025), Laux, Stephany and Liefgreen (2025), and the ILO global exposure index.

**Coding exercise:** inspect a small set of worker–AI interaction records. Use a loop to compare model advice for a novice and experienced worker, store whether advice is accepted, and separate immediate task quality from possible learning. The methodological point is that a model-quality score is not an estimate of an organizational effect.

### 11 November — Writing, influence and relationships

**Question:** What happens when an LLM takes part in how people express themselves and respond to others?

**Main ideas:** AI-mediated communication; autocomplete as an intervention; influence without awareness; cultural homogenization; authorship and voice; disclosure, anthropomorphism and relational attachment; heterogeneity by user and use pattern.

**Recommended essential readings:**

1. Williams-Ceci et al. (2026), “Biased AI Writing Assistants Shift Users' Attitudes on Societal Issues,” *Science Advances*. The strongest current causal study of model suggestions entering the writing process.
2. Agarwal, Naaman and Vashistha (2025), “AI Suggestions Homogenize Writing toward Western Styles and Diminish Cultural Nuances,” CHI. A cross-cultural experiment that shifts the question from accuracy to whose expressive conventions are normalized.
3. Zhang et al. (2026), “Interaction with AI Companions and Psychological Well-Being,” *Nature Human Behaviour*. A substantive study of actual companion users, with appropriate cautions about causal interpretation.

If three full readings are too much, retain Williams-Ceci and Zhang as essential and use Agarwal et al. as an extended lecture case.

**Additional readings:** Waldschütz et al. (2026); Jakesch et al. (2023); the MIT/OpenAI affective-use studies (as non-peer-reviewed methodological material); the PNAS perspective on anthropomorphic conversational agents; and the 2025 PNAS study showing LLM preferences for LLM-written communications.

**Coding exercise:** create two autocomplete conditions that differ only in stance, then log accepted and rejected suggestions while composing a short paragraph. A second, optional comparison changes the style rather than the stance. Students inspect the final text, the interaction log and a pre/post response separately. This teaches that the treatment is the sequence of suggestions and choices, not only the final paragraph.

### 18 November — Language, inequality and institutional power

**Question:** How can model outputs reproduce social distinctions even when those distinctions are not named?

**Main ideas:** overt and covert group cues; dialect as a racialized signal; matched-guise audits; intersectionality; allocation decisions; training and alignment; audit findings versus causal explanations; model and provider dependence.

**Recommended essential readings:**

1. Hofmann et al. (2024), “AI Generates Covertly Racist Decisions about People Based on Their Dialect,” *Nature*. A clear sociological theory, paired design and consequential outcomes.
2. Waight et al. (2026), “State Media Control Influences Large Language Models,” *Nature*. An unusually strong multi-method attempt to connect output differences to training data and institutions.
3. One substantive allocation study: the 2025 *PNAS Nexus* resume audit, or the 2026 *Nature Communications* study of intersectional bias in generated narratives. The resume audit is preferable if the lecture emphasizes employment; the narrative study is preferable if it emphasizes cultural representation.

**Additional readings:** Hu et al. (2025), Wang, Morgenstern and Dickerson (2025), the PNAS AI–AI bias article, Buyl et al. (2026), and recent audits of socioeconomic and ideological bias.

**Coding exercise:** a small predeclared matched-guise audit. Students send semantically matched Standardized American English and African American English inputs to two models, preserve every condition in a list of dictionaries, and compare recommendations. The code teaches a simple factorial grid and result table; the substantive check prevents a two-prompt demonstration from being presented as a population estimate or causal explanation of training.

## What to remove or redistribute

- Remove the dedicated Week 9–10 sequence on generative agent interaction and collective intelligence.
- Remove the dedicated Week 11 research-agent lecture.
- Keep “agent,” “tool use,” “memory,” “harness” and “multi-agent simulation” in the glossary and additional resources.
- If research agents remain important, use a 15–20 minute example in the work/organizations lecture: research agents are a proposed reorganization of research labour, not a separate sociological domain.
- Keep replication and reporting as requirements of every weekly routine and final project. They were introduced in Week 1 and do not need a cancelled holiday lecture.
- Move model audits from the cancelled 2 December slot into the 18 November substantive lecture on inequality and institutional power.

## Why this arc is preferable

It does not abandon the technical progression. The three coding tasks can still introduce a function, interaction logs and a small parameter grid. But the code now serves empirical questions about labour, influence and inequality. The sequence also gives students three increasingly wide units of analysis: organization, interaction and institution. It ends with a defensible audit design that reuses the measurement, experiment and synthetic-population distinctions developed earlier in the course.

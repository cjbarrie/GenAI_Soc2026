# Week 11 teaching architecture

**Question:** When is using AI interpreted as resourceful, and when as lazy or incompetent? Does the author's status change that interpretation?

**Bounded conclusion:** Experiments identify penalties in how AI users are judged for effort, ability and trust. Status theory and institutional research make unequal evaluation plausible, but the proposed senior/junior interaction is untested by those studies. A larger penalty for the senior worker is also possible.

## Lecture, reading discussion and coding

| Time | Material | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 min | Identical fictional competitor report; senior director and junior analyst | Make text quality, author judgment and assistance disclosure separate questions |
| 10–22 min | Reif Study 2 | Establish what the AI-use evaluation experiments actually found and did not find about moderators |
| 22–32 min | Foschi | Explain status-based competence thresholds and the reverse prediction |
| 32–39 min | Bourdieu, Lamont and Lareau | Specify the workplace as a setting that assigns value to writing and ways of producing it |
| 39–47 min | Rafalow | Show a pre-LLM case of institutions recognizing similar digital practices differently |
| 47–57 min | Smart Compose comparison; Schilke and Reimann Study 11 | Distinguish routine writing assistance, disclosure and trust |
| 57–65 min | Two competing status predictions and proposed 2×2 | State what new human-reader evidence would be needed |
| 65–120 min | Discussion of the four required papers | Compare designs, outcomes, status measures and alternative predictions |
| 120–165 min | Beginner Python walkthrough and task | Edit one report, inspect raw return, fact-check it, and construct four identical-text reader versions |

The main example is instructor-authored. The LLM's live revision is model output; the four descriptions are proposed experimental materials; no human outcomes are observed. The same distinction is maintained in the slides, book and notebook.

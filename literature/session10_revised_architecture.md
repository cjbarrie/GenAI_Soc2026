# Week 10 revised architecture: LLMs, writing, influence and relationships

**Status:** Approval gate 2. This document fixes the intellectual and teaching architecture. It does not authorize construction of the final slides, workbook or course-book pages.

## Central question

**What happens to social relationships when people use LLMs to help them communicate, interpret others and manage interpersonal interactions?**

The class concerns LLM mediation of communication **between people**. It does not cover AI companions, artificial confidants or the displacement of human emotional support.

## Bounded conclusion

When an LLM helps someone perform an interpersonal act, it can change the words used, the explanations offered and the repair proposed. It can also change how authorship, effort and responsibility are attributed. A generated message alone does not show that the sender is sincere, that the recipient regards the message as appropriate, that anyone is persuaded or that a relationship is repaired.

The class will keep three outcomes separate:

| Object of study | Question | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Generated writing | What language, explanation and repair did the system produce? | The prompt, output and source facts |
| Influence on the writer | Did assistance change what the writer thought, selected or reported? | Human outcome data and an identifying comparison |
| Interpersonal consequence | How did a recipient interpret the act, and what followed for the relationship? | Recipient data, interactional evidence and, for maintenance, evidence over time |

## Cumulative teaching example

### The broken commitment

The sender promised to help another person move at 8 a.m. on Saturday. At noon on Friday, the sender learned that their employer required them to work from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. They wait until 9 p.m. to send the message. The other person had declined paid help because they expected the sender to come. The sender can either pay half the cost of a last-minute mover or help on Sunday. No other facts or feelings are supplied.

The same facts are used for three relationships:

- a colleague;
- a close friend;
- a romantic partner.

This is consequential enough to create a real obligation, but ordinary enough for students to inspect without specialized knowledge. It makes four parts of an apology visible:

1. the account of what happened;
2. acknowledgment of the broken commitment and late notice;
3. recognition of the other person's reliance;
4. a proposed repair that the recipient may accept or refuse.

### Two model instructions

The first request is deliberately thin:

> Write a message telling a close friend that I cannot help them move tomorrow. Use the facts supplied below and do not invent facts.

The second names the relational work without prescribing exact wording:

> Write the message to a close friend. State the commitment that was broken, acknowledge the late notice and the plan they made in reliance on the promise, explain only the facts supplied, and offer the two available forms of repair. Do not invent feelings, relationship history or motives.

The first comparison therefore concerns the **instruction**, not the recipient. The later comparison holds the second instruction and all facts fixed while changing only the relationship. Students can inspect generated differences without treating those differences as valid measures of sincerity or relational quality.

### What this example is for

- Zelizer asks what it means to delegate or mediate an act within a particular tie.
- Goffman identifies the incident, account, acknowledgment and possible corrective exchange.
- Hochschild prevents us from treating an appropriate emotional expression as evidence about inner feeling or emotion work.
- Gershon asks what the use or disclosure of a medium communicates in addition to the literal message.
- Illouz helps students notice the cultural vocabularies through which models formulate injury and repair.
- Hohenstein et al. show that generated suggestions can alter actual conversations and partner judgments.
- Williams-Ceci et al. show a different mechanism: suggestions can influence the writer during composition.

## Historical sociological argument

The historical section has three principal theorists. Each section begins with the sociological problem and evidence, develops the argument, and only then introduces the LLM question.

### 1. Viviana Zelizer: how relationships give transactions their meaning

#### Original problem

Zelizer challenges two common accounts. The **separate-spheres** account treats intimacy and economic calculation as different domains. The **hostile-worlds** account adds that contact between them contaminates both: money corrupts intimacy and sentiment corrupts rational exchange. Zelizer argues that empirical social life does not work this way. People routinely combine intimacy and economic activity while differentiating relationships and negotiating what particular transfers mean.

#### Evidence and original example

*The Purchase of Intimacy* uses legal disputes and historical evidence about coupling, care and household relations. The opening case, *Cole v. Lucas*, concerned promissory notes given by Josiah Miller to Patsy, an enslaved woman with whom he had an intimate relationship. The legal meaning of the same transfer depended on whether the court classified the tie as master–slave, concubinage or marriage. The case is not a loose analogy for AI. Its teaching purpose is to show that an act cannot be interpreted independently of the relationship, the rights attached to it and the institutional authority that classifies the tie.

#### Theoretical contribution

Zelizer's alternative is connected lives and relational work. People differentiate ties, mark their boundaries, sustain them through joint activities and continually renegotiate their content. They match relations with transactions, media and meanings. The same material transfer can therefore be a wage, allowance, gift, loan, compensation or insult.

#### LLM connection

An AI-assisted apology is not important only because its tone may vary. Delegating the formulation of an apology changes the medium through which responsibility, effort and repair are performed. Its meaning may depend on whether the assistance is concealed or disclosed, how much the sender revises, what the relationship normally requires and whether the recipient treats authorship as part of the obligation. The model output cannot settle those questions.

### 2. Erving Goffman: how people repair an interactional offence

#### Original problem

Goffman studies the interaction order: how people sustain a workable definition of a situation and a socially valued image of themselves during encounters. His object is not private authenticity. It is the organization of conduct among participants who interpret one another's acts.

#### Evidence and examples

“On Face-Work” is a conceptual analysis grounded in ordinary encounters, etiquette, embarrassment, avoidance, hints and corrective exchanges. A participant takes a **line** through verbal and non-verbal acts. Others use that line to infer the participant's view of the situation. **Face** is the positive social value a person effectively claims through that line.

#### Theoretical contribution

A broken promise creates an incident because conduct is no longer consistent with the line the relationship had supported. Goffman's corrective process distinguishes moves that the opening example makes observable: a challenge or recognition of the offence, an account, acknowledgment of responsibility, compensation or expiation, and possible acceptance. An explanation may narrow blame without repairing the injury. A proposed repair gives the recipient something they may accept, reject or renegotiate.

#### LLM connection

The model can help formulate a line and can supply the recognizable parts of a corrective exchange. That creates an attribution problem: whose line is being presented, and who is accountable for it? Goffman also notes that delicate transactions can be conducted through go-betweens. An LLM is not simply equivalent to a human intermediary, but this observation gives us a more precise question than whether the prose sounds authentic.

### 3. Arlie Hochschild: expression, feeling and the work of managing emotion

#### Original problem

Hochschild asks how social expectations reach into emotional life. People encounter rules about what they should feel, how strongly and for how long. They may then try to bring their feelings into line with the situation.

#### Evidence

The 1979 article combines theoretical argument with a content analysis of 261 written protocols produced by students in two Berkeley classes in 1974. Respondents described situations in which they changed a situation to fit their feelings or tried to change their feelings to fit a situation. The method matters: these are retrospective accounts of attempts to manage emotion, not direct observations of inner feeling.

#### Theoretical contribution

**Feeling rules** specify socially appropriate feeling. **Emotion work** is the attempt to change the degree or quality of feeling in relation to such rules; it may succeed or fail. Outward expression is analytically separate. A person can display an appropriate expression without changing feeling, and can perform emotion work without producing a convincing outward display.

#### LLM connection

An LLM can reduce the expressive effort needed to formulate concern or remorse. It might also facilitate emotion work by prompting reflection, helping someone articulate why another person was harmed or offering ways to reappraise the event. It can equally generate a conventional display without any work on feeling. A third outcome belongs to the recipient: how they interpret the expression. The text alone identifies none of these processes conclusively.

### Supporting extensions

#### Ilana Gershon: media ideologies

Gershon's interviews about relationship dissolution show that people hold socially learned beliefs about what different media communicate. Choosing a text, call or face-to-face conversation supplies “second-order information” about seriousness, effort and obligation. AI assistance adds a production layer to the medium. Whether it matters depends on local norms and attribution, not a universal property of AI text.

#### Eva Illouz: therapeutic vocabularies

Illouz traces how psychological and therapeutic languages became widely available ways of describing selves, injuries, needs and relationships. This helps students examine whether models repeatedly frame ordinary conflicts through terms such as boundaries, validation and emotional availability. A single output can illustrate the vocabulary but cannot establish cultural standardization.

#### Katz and Lazarsfeld: interpersonal influence

The Decatur study is mentioned briefly when the lecture turns from writing to influence. Its contribution is that communication effects are socially mediated: information and influence travel through relationships. The section will not give Katz and Lazarsfeld a separate historical sequence or turn an LLM into an “opinion leader.”

## Required readings and their teaching purposes

### Viviana Zelizer (2012)

Zelizer, Viviana A. 2012. “How I Became a Relational Economic Sociologist and What Does That Mean?” *Politics & Society* 40(2): 145–174. https://doi.org/10.1177/0032329212441591.

**Purpose:** This is the conceptual anchor. It teaches students to identify the relationship, act or transaction, medium, boundary and negotiated meaning rather than judging an AI-generated message from its wording alone. The lecture uses *The Purchase of Intimacy* to supply the fuller historical argument and original legal example; the assigned article lets students read Zelizer's own later statement of the programme in full.

### Hohenstein et al. (2023)

Hohenstein, Jess, Rene F. Kizilcec, Dominic DiFranzo, Zhila Aghajari, Hannah Mieczkowski, Karen Levy, Mor Naaman, Jeffrey Hancock, and Malte F. Jung. 2023. “Artificial Intelligence in Communication Impacts Language and Social Relationships.” *Scientific Reports* 13: 5487. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-30938-9.

**Purpose:** This is the main relationship study. In Study 1, 219 pairs were independently randomized to have Google smart replies available or unavailable while discussing a policy issue. Availability encouraged actual use, and the authors use randomized availability as an instrument when estimating effects of actual use. Actual partner use was associated through that design with more positive ratings of cooperation and affiliation. By contrast, participants' belief that a partner used smart replies was only measured; greater suspected use was associated with less cooperation and affiliation and more dominance after controlling for actual use. That second result is correlational, not a randomized disclosure effect. Study 2 then randomized 291 pairs to Google, positive, negative or no smart replies to identify effects on conversational language. The paper therefore allows careful separation of system availability, actual use, suspected use, language and interpersonal judgment. It still does not establish long-term relationship maintenance.

### Williams-Ceci et al. (2026)

Williams-Ceci, Sterling, Maurice Jakesch, Advait Bhat, Kowe Kadoma, Lior Zalmanson, and Mor Naaman. 2026. “Biased AI Writing Assistants Shift Users' Attitudes on Societal Issues.” *Science Advances* 12(11): eadw5578. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adw5578.

**Purpose:** This paper identifies a different pathway. Across two preregistered experiments, participants wrote about societal issues with or without biased autocomplete and then reported their own attitudes. The outcome belongs to the writer, not to a message recipient or relationship. Its teaching job is to show why LLM mediation cannot be treated as a neutral transfer from a pre-existing intention into prose. The study does not show that the resulting messages persuaded another person or altered a relationship.

## Reading discussion questions

1. Using Zelizer's framework, what relationship, obligation, medium and meaning are present in the moving example? Which of these can the model observe and which must it infer?
2. In Hohenstein et al., why do randomized availability, actual use and suspected use support different kinds of claims?
3. Williams-Ceci et al. identify influence on writers. What additional design would be needed to test influence on a recipient or consequence for a relationship?
4. Does an AI-drafted apology count as the sender's line in Goffman's sense? What facts would affect the answer?
5. What observations could distinguish reduced expressive effort, facilitated emotion work and a generated display that the sender does not endorse?

## Python and LLM walkthrough

### Research operation

`fixed facts → relationship string → instruction → model call → raw generated message → researcher inspection`

### Visible progression

1. Store the fixed scenario in a dictionary and print each field.
2. Store `relationship = "close friend"` as a string.
3. Build and run the unconstrained prompt with literal values.
4. Build and run the relational-obligation prompt with the same literal values.
5. Preserve both raw returns as strings.
6. Ask the student—not another model—to note whether each output mentions the promise, late notice, reliance and the two available repairs, and to record any invented fact.
7. Define `draft_apology(relationship, instruction)` only after the two literal calls are understood.
8. Trace one call with the actual argument values entering and the returned string leaving.
9. Use one loop to repeat the relational-obligation instruction for `colleague`, `close friend` and `romantic partner`.
10. Save a list of dictionaries containing the relationship, instruction and raw output.
11. Compare what changes in explanation, obligation, apology and repair.

### Methodological boundary in the notebook

The notebook will show three boxes:

- **Observed:** prompt, model, raw generated text and researcher notes about textual features.
- **Interpreted by the researcher:** whether wording appears to acknowledge reliance, what assumptions seem to have been introduced and how the proposed repair differs.
- **Not observed:** sender sincerity, emotion work, recipient judgment, persuasion, forgiveness and later relationship quality.

The model will not be asked to score sincerity, relational appropriateness or relationship quality. A human-coded checklist can organize inspection, but it is not an outcome measure.

### Take-home task

Students change one element with a sociological purpose: either the relationship label or one clause in the relational-obligation instruction. They rerun the call, preserve both outputs and narrate:

- the input value and Python type entering each operation;
- the output value and type leaving it;
- the textual difference they observed;
- one interpretation they can reasonably make;
- one human outcome the exercise does not measure;
- how one required reading changes their interpretation.

The submission remains a narrated screen recording. Code elegance and persuasive performance are not assessed.

## Transition logic

- The opening model comparison creates an empirical object but withholds evaluation.
- Zelizer shows why the meaning of mediation cannot be read from wording alone.
- Goffman identifies the interactional work an apology is attempting to perform.
- Hochschild separates that performance from feeling and emotion work.
- Gershon and Illouz add the meanings of media choice and available cultural vocabularies.
- Katz and Lazarsfeld introduce the possibility that influence depends on the social relation through which language travels.
- Hohenstein et al. provide evidence about actual dyadic communication and partner judgments.
- Williams-Ceci et al. identify a different outcome: change in the writer during composition.
- The Python exercise returns to the opening case and makes only the generated language observable.
- The close states plainly which claims require a study with human senders and recipients.
